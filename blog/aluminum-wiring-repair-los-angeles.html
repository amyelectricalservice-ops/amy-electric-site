01 · Why LA homes have it

Why Los Angeles Homes Still Have Aluminum Wiring

In the mid-1960s the price of copper climbed sharply, and builders across Southern California switched to cheaper aluminum branch wiring for their 120-volt circuits. Aluminum wire was used in the postwar tract housing that fills the San Fernando Valley, Pasadena, Altadena, Burbank and Glendale. It was installed at one gauge size larger than copper, so a 15-amp circuit that would have carried 14 AWG copper carried 12 AWG solid aluminum.

The alloy matters. The AA-1350 series used in the mid-1960s was soft and prone to breaking at stress points. In 1972 the AA-8000 series replaced it, and post-1972 aluminum installed correctly performs much like copper. That is why home inspectors flag pre-1972 solid aluminum and treat later work with more caution than the internet suggests.

The wiring itself usually outlives the problem. Almost all of the risk lives in the last inch of the wire, where aluminum lands on a screw terminal.

02 · Identification

How to Tell If Your Home Has Aluminum Wiring

You do not need to open the walls to find it. Aluminum is stamped on the cable jacket itself, every few feet along its length. Look at the printed or embossed markings on the sheath where it is visible: in an unfinished basement, attic, garage, or at the electrical panel. Use a flashlight at a low angle across the jacket so the lettering reads clearly.

  • "AL" or "Aluminum" on the jacket means aluminum conductors. A jacket that also reads "CU-clad" or "Copper-clad" means copper-coated aluminum, which is a different animal and rarely the same problem.
  • Solid versus stranded matters. Stranded aluminum is common, still in everyday use, and rarely fails. Solid aluminum on 15- and 20-amp circuits is the wiring that gets reported.
  • Check the panel first. Inspectors usually spot it by removing the dead front of the service equipment, which is also where a licensed electrician confirms what you have.
  • One circuit is enough. A 1950s house finished in 1970 can have solid aluminum in the new circuits and copper everywhere else. If any solid aluminum is present, the whole circuit set needs evaluation.
03 · The risk

The Risk Is at the Connections, Not the Wire

Aluminum behaves differently from copper in three ways that matter at a terminal. It expands and contracts far more as a circuit heats and cools, which loosens connections over time, a behavior called creep. It oxidizes quickly when exposed to air, and aluminum oxide does not conduct, so a corroded connection loses conductivity quietly. And it is soft, so over-tightening crushes the conductor and creates a weak point that can break.

A loose connection can micro-arc. That arcing runs hot enough to melt insulation, expose the conductor and start a fire inside a wall cavity. The part that makes this worth taking seriously: failing aluminum connections seldom give you a useful warning. They have been documented failing and overheating with no prior indication, which is why an inspection report flags the wiring even when nothing has gone wrong yet.

Signs worth calling about, when they appear:

  • Face plates at outlets or switches that are hot to the touch or discolored
  • Flickering lights, or lights that dim when another appliance starts
  • Circuits that work sometimes and not others, or outlets that go dead
  • A smell of burning plastic near outlets or switches
  • Breakers that trip repeatedly on the same circuit
  • A faint buzzing from a receptacle that stops when you jostle the outlet

A burning smell, sparking or a hot outlet is an emergency. Cut the circuit and call an electrician the same day.

Approved Repairs

The Three Permanent Repairs the CPSC Approves

The Consumer Product Safety Commission reviewed proposed repair methods against three tests: safe, effective and permanent. Only three methods pass, and knowing which one you are being offered matters more than the price on the quote.

🔧

1. Replace with copper cable

The complete solution. Every aluminum conductor is removed and replaced with copper, which eliminates the failure mode at its source. It is also the most invasive and the most expensive option, and it is the right call when the wiring is damaged or the house is being gutted anyway.

🔌

2. COPALUM pigtailing

A short copper pigtail is crimped onto the end of each aluminum wire with a dedicated crimp tool, then landed on the device terminal. It is a CPSC-approved permanent repair, and only installers trained and equipped with the crimp tool can do it properly.

⚙️

3. AlumiConn connectors

A set-screw connector joins the copper pigtail to the aluminum wire. The CPSC lists it as the acceptable alternative when COPALUM work is not available. The screws must be torqued to the manufacturer's specification, and the connector takes more space in a box than a pigtail alone.

What is not on that list matters too. Pigtailing with ordinary twist-on wire connectors was evaluated by the CPSC and deemed a temporary repair; a substantial number of those connectors overheated, and the method does not solve the overheating problem it was meant to fix. Other crimp connectors sold at hardware stores have never been evaluated for copper-to-aluminum work. And copper-to-aluminum terminals must be devices rated for the combination, marked CO/ALR or AL-CU, with brass screws.

One more point of clarity: aluminum branch-circuit wiring is not illegal. The residential code permits aluminum conductors, and existing installations are not required to be removed. What the code does not permit is leaving a deteriorated connection in place, or making a new connection with hardware that was never listed for the job.

Permits

Permits, Inspection, and Who Can Do the Work

Aluminum branch-circuit work in Los Angeles is permitted electrical work. LADBS is the City of Los Angeles building department, and it runs permitting online through PermitLA and ePlanLA; other cities in Greater LA have their own building departments with the same requirement. A city inspection verifies the work against the California Electrical Code before the job closes.

Access inside the service panel is restricted by local rules, and the work itself needs a licensed C-10 contractor. A COPALUM repair in particular needs a trained installer with the crimp tool; a mis-terminated pigtail can look finished and still be the weak point it was meant to fix.

Before any of it starts, we inspect the panel, the device boxes, and the service entrance. If the aluminum reaches the service conductors or the panel bus, that changes the scope, and a panel upgrade often belongs in the same project.

Inspections & Insurance

Inspections, Insurance, and What Happens at Escrow

Home inspectors are required to report solid aluminum branch-circuit wiring when they can see it. The InterNACHI home inspection standard says the inspector shall report the presence of solid conductor aluminum branch-circuit wiring as in need of correction, and that solid aluminum confirmed or suspected of being installed before 1972 should be recommended for evaluation by a licensed electrician. That report is what turns an electrical curiosity into a scheduled project.

How much risk sits behind the report is worth stating precisely. A 2011 study funded by the CPSC and carried out by the Franklin Institute found that homes built before 1972 and wired with aluminum were 55 times more likely to have one or more wire connections at outlets reach fire hazard conditions than homes wired with copper. That survey covered outlet connections only, and the finding is routinely quoted more broadly than the study supports. It is a real risk factor at the outlets, not a verdict on every circuit in the house.

Insurance is where it gets personal. Some carriers will not write a policy for a home with solid aluminum branch wiring, and others write it with special conditions and additional cost. Your carrier decides, and the answer changes by company and by property, so ask yours directly rather than assuming. A permitted repair with an inspection record is the document both your insurer and a buyer's agent want to see.

Decision

Repair or Rewire: How to Decide

Most homes with aluminum wiring do not need a full rewire. The decision comes down to condition and plans:

  • The wiring is intact and only some devices show wear: connector remediation with COPALUM or AlumiConn at the affected outlets, switches, fixtures and panel terminations is the approved permanent fix, and it avoids opening walls.
  • The aluminum is damaged, or connections have failed repeatedly: replacing those circuits with copper is the only durable answer.
  • The house is being renovated or gutted: rewire while the walls are open. It costs less in that state than in any other, and the drywall repair is already in the budget.
  • You are adding load, an EV charger, or solar: a modern panel usually needs to be part of the same project anyway, and our panel upgrade cost guide covers what that adds.

For homes where only part of the wiring is affected, a partial rewire is the middle path, and it is where most of our aluminum work lands.

Questions

Frequently Asked Questions About Aluminum Wiring

Do I have to replace aluminum wiring in my Los Angeles home? ▾
Not always. If the solid aluminum conductors are intact, the CPSC-approved permanent fixes are connector remediation at the affected outlets, switches, fixtures and panel terminations, using COPALUM crimp connectors or AlumiConn connectors. Full copper replacement is the answer when the wiring is damaged, connections keep failing, or the house is being renovated with the walls open. Which one your home needs comes down to condition, and that is what an on-site inspection is for.
How much does aluminum wiring repair cost in Los Angeles? ▾
Aluminum wiring replacement adds 20 to 40 percent to a rewire baseline. A partial rewire covering the affected circuits runs $3,000 to $8,000, and a 100A to 200A panel upgrade runs $2,500 to $4,500. Rewiring permit and inspection fees run $300 to $800, panel permit and inspection $200 to $450, and drywall repair after a rewire $2,000 to $8,000. Connector remediation at individual devices is priced per connection once we know how many are affected. Every estimate is free and provided in writing.
Is aluminum wiring illegal in California? ▾
No. The residential code permits aluminum branch-circuit conductors, and existing installations are not required to be removed. What the code does not allow is leaving a deteriorated connection in place, or making a new connection with hardware that was never listed for copper-to-aluminum work. Devices joined to aluminum must be rated for the combination, marked CO/ALR or AL-CU. Repairs and additions still require a permit and an inspection.
What is the difference between COPALUM and AlumiConn? ▾
Both are CPSC-approved permanent repairs, and both pigtail a short piece of copper onto the aluminum conductor. COPALUM uses a dedicated crimp tool to cold-weld the pigtail to the aluminum, and only trained installers with that tool can do it properly. AlumiConn uses a set-screw connector tightened to the manufacturer's torque specification, and it is the acceptable alternative when COPALUM work is not available. It takes more space in a box.
Does aluminum wiring affect home insurance or selling my home? ▾
It can. Home inspectors are required to report solid aluminum branch-circuit wiring as in need of correction, and solid aluminum suspected of being installed before 1972 should be evaluated by a licensed electrician. Some insurers will not write a policy on a home with solid aluminum branch wiring, and others write it with special conditions and added cost, so ask your carrier directly. At escrow, a permitted repair with an inspection record is the document the insurer and the buyer's agent ask for.
Do I need a permit for aluminum wiring repair in Los Angeles? ▾
Yes. Aluminum branch-circuit work is permitted electrical work. LADBS is the City of Los Angeles building department and runs its permitting online through PermitLA and ePlanLA; other cities in Greater LA have their own building departments with the same requirement. A city inspection verifies the work against the California Electrical Code before the job closes.
