When to Book

When You Actually Need an Electrical Inspection in LA

Inspections are not an annual subscription for most homes. They are a decision tool, and these are the situations where the $200–$400 buys something real:

  • Buying a home. A general home inspector looks at the panel from three feet away. An electrician opens it, tests under load, and reads the wiring methods. On pre-1970 LA housing stock this routinely changes the negotiation.
  • Selling a home. A pre-listing inspection lets you fix or disclose defects on your schedule instead of the buyer's.
  • Before adding a large load. EV charger, heat pump, pool equipment, or a kitchen remodel all depend on panel capacity. A load calculation plus inspection is cheaper than discovering the limit after purchase.
  • Insurance or lender request. Carriers increasingly ask for a panel photo or an electrician's letter on older services, particularly Zinsco and Federal Pacific panels.
  • After a renovation or a flood. Water intrusion and unpermitted additions both leave evidence that only shows up under inspection.
  • Symptoms you cannot explain. Warm outlet covers, a burning smell, flickering on one circuit, or breakers that trip without a clear cause.
The Report

What a Good Inspection Report Looks Like

An inspection should end with a written report you can act on. Ours has four parts: what was tested, what was found, how urgent each finding is, and what the code basis is. Photos of anything flagged go in the report — a verbal "you need a new panel" is not a finding.

  • Findings ranked by urgency: immediate hazard, code violation to correct at next opportunity, and observation.
  • Code references so a buyer, seller, or insurer can verify the basis instead of trusting a sales pitch.
  • Measured data: voltage drop under load, breaker temperatures, GFCI trip times, ground resistance where relevant.
  • Separate repair pricing — the inspection is not a sales funnel. You get the report whether or not we do the work.

What an inspection is not: it is not a home inspection, not a permit inspection by the building department, and not a guarantee that concealed wiring is sound. It is a professional assessment of everything visible and testable on the day of the visit.

Questions

Electrical Inspection FAQ

How much does an electrical inspection cost in Los Angeles? ▾
A general home safety inspection costs $150–$300. Pre-purchase inspections for home buyers run $200–$400, and panel-only inspections cost $100–$200. Thermal imaging and aluminum wiring assessments are add-ons that cost $100–$400 additional. Most inspections take 1–3 hours.
What does an electrical safety inspection cover? ▾
A licensed electrician inspects your electrical panel (overheating, breaker sizing, grounding), tests outlets and GFCI devices, checks visible wiring in attics and crawlspaces, verifies smoke and CO alarm placement, evaluates the grounding electrode system, and checks outdoor electrical for GFCI protection and weatherproof covers.
How often should I have my electrical system inspected? ▾
Every 3–5 years for modern homes, and annually for homes built before 1980 or those with aluminum wiring. You should also get an inspection before buying or selling a home, after any major renovation, or if you notice warning signs like flickering lights, warm outlets, or frequent breaker trips.
Does a home inspection include the electrical system? ▾
Only at a visual level. A general home inspector checks that the panel exists, notes obvious double-taps, and tests a sample of outlets with a plug-in tester. They do not open the panel dead-front, run load calculations, test under load, or identify aluminum branch wiring. On older Los Angeles homes that gap is where the expensive surprises live, which is why buyers order a dedicated electrical inspection.
Can an electrical inspection fail? ▾
No. An inspection is an assessment, not a pass/fail test, and there is no score. The report ranks findings by urgency: immediate hazards, code violations to correct, and observations. A building-department permit inspection is different, and that one can fail if the work does not meet code.
Do you fix what the inspection finds? ▾
We can, but the report is yours either way and we price repairs separately with itemized line items. You are free to get a second bid. We do not withhold findings to sell work, and we do not quote repairs before the inspection is complete.
