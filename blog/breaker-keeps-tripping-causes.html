Why Does My Breaker Keep Tripping?
Causes & Fixes

By AMY Electric, Licensed C-10 #981578 · EVITP #4051604

A circuit breaker that trips is telling you something. Sometimes it is a simple overload. Sometimes it is the only warning before damaged wiring creates a fire or shock hazard. This guide covers the six causes, the one safe reset, what repairs cost in Los Angeles, and when to stop troubleshooting and call.

Start safely

What a Tripping Breaker Is Designed to Do

A breaker opens the circuit when current exceeds a safe level, or when protective electronics detect an arc or ground fault. It protects the conductors inside your walls, not just the appliance at the end of the circuit. A trip is therefore useful information, not an inconvenience to defeat.

Before touching anything, look and listen. Smoke, flames, a sharp burning odor, buzzing, a warm panel, visible scorching, water near electrical equipment, or anyone receiving a shock changes this from troubleshooting to an emergency. Leave the area and call 911 for fire or injury, or request same-day electrical repair.

Common causes

Six Reasons Breakers Trip

The first three explain most trips. The last three are the ones homeowners usually miss.

1. Circuit overload

A 15- or 20-amp branch circuit carries only so much. Portable heaters, hair dryers, microwaves, irons, vacuums, and window air conditioners run together will overload one. In older Los Angeles homes, one circuit may serve more rooms than the owner realizes, often after past renovations regrouped the wiring without redrawing the directory.

2. Short circuit

A damaged hot conductor touching neutral creates a sudden current surge. A crushed cord, failed receptacle, loose terminal, or nail through a cable can be involved. The breaker may trip instantly when a device is plugged in or switched on, sometimes with a visible scorch mark or a burning smell.

3. Ground fault

Current escaping to a metal box, grounding conductor, wet surface, or appliance frame trips a GFCI or AFCI device. Kitchens, bathrooms, garages, patios, pools, and laundry areas deserve extra caution because moisture increases shock risk, not just trip frequency.

4. Arc fault

Loose connections, damaged insulation, or a failing device can create an electrical arc that an AFCI breaker is designed to detect. Do not swap or bypass an AFCI to stop the nuisance trips. They may be signaling a real wiring or appliance defect that the breaker is correctly reporting.

5. Failing breaker or panel

Breakers wear out, fail to latch, or develop heat damage over decades of service. A panel with corrosion, double-tapped conductors, obsolete equipment, or a history of overheating needs a professional inspection rather than a larger breaker. Los Angeles also still has its share of manufactured hazard brands; if a trip brings flickering or a charred smell, our burning smell and panel triage guide covers the escalation.

6. New load exceeds available capacity

Adding an EV charger, heat-pump equipment, an induction range, a spa, or workshop tools can expose a capacity problem that older wiring never had to carry. An electrician should run a load calculation before adding the circuit, and a panel upgrade may be the right answer, though it is not the fix for every trip.

Diagnose yourself

How to Diagnose a Tripping Breaker Safely

  1. Identify the affected area. Note which rooms, outlets, lights, or appliances lost power. Check whether a GFCI receptacle itself tripped, and reset it only if it is dry and undamaged.
  2. Unplug everything on the affected circuit: heaters, cooking appliances, tools, power strips, chargers. Do not move a suspected damaged appliance to another outlet to test it.
  3. Reset once. Push the handle firmly to OFF first. Tripped breakers rest in a middle position and will not re-engage from there, so a timid flick back to ON fails confusingly. Then flip fully to ON, standing to the side with dry hands. If it trips immediately, stop.
  4. Restore loads one by one. Plug devices back in a single at a time. The one that trips the circuit identified its fault, or its draw with everything else already running identified the overload.
  5. Read the pattern. A trip that happens minutes into high-draw use points toward an overload or heat at a loose connection. An instant trip when one specific device starts points toward a short or ground fault on that device. Either pattern can require instruments to confirm.
  6. Document clues. Time of day, weather, what was running, any odor or sound. It shortens the diagnostic visit and often the bill.

Never tape, wedge, or hold a breaker on, and never replace it with a higher-amperage one. The breaker is sized to the wire gauge behind it, and oversizing converts a nuisance trip into overheated conductors inside your walls. Do not remove the panel cover: parts inside can remain energized even with the main breaker off.

If the breaker trips again with no load connected, or will not reset at all, leave it off and schedule diagnosis. That combination is a short, a ground fault, or a failed breaker, and none of the three improve on their own.

Professional diagnosis

How an Electrician Finds the Fault

Diagnosis starts with a visual inspection and the trip pattern you documented: what tripped, when, and what was running. From there the tests cover what the pattern suggests. Voltage, continuity, and insulation tests narrow the fault location. GFCI and AFCI performance testing distinguishes legitimate trips from worn devices. Thermal imaging reveals abnormal heat behind the dead front of a panel or at a loose connection. Circuit tracing establishes which outlets and fixtures share the breaker, which matters in older Los Angeles homes more than most owners expect.

The fix can be as focused as replacing a damaged receptacle or the cord of a device with an internal fault, or as extensive as correcting a loose splice, separating overloaded loads, rewiring a branch circuit, or replacing a heat-damaged panel. The correct repair preserves the conductor size and the protective-device rating, because both are a matched pair. For permitted work, requirements depend on the city and the scope. In the City of Los Angeles, permit and inspection expectations are confirmed with LADBS; any permitted breaker or circuit work is verified by a city inspection before the job closes.

Where the trip is one symptom of an older wiring system, a broader rewiring evaluation may be recommended without assuming the entire house must be rewired. Our aluminum wiring guide covers the most common case in 1960s and 1970s Los Angeles homes.

Patterns

Patterns That Help Explain a Trip

Pay attention to whether the problem follows one appliance or stays with one outlet. If a portable device trips multiple known-good circuits, stop using it and have the device serviced or replaced. If several different devices work elsewhere but one receptacle trips the circuit, the receptacle, its box, or the branch wiring is the subject. Do not keep moving a questionable appliance around the house to "test" it; that is the fastest way to rerun the same fault through different circuits.

Seasonal conditions change the pattern. Summer air conditioners and pool equipment add long-running loads. Winter heaters and holiday lighting add temporary demand. In coastal neighborhoods, moisture and corrosion affect outdoor receptacles and can turn a device that was fine last year into a recurring trip. Renovations in older homes leave circuits mislabeled or merged; a corrected circuit directory after a repair is a small item that makes the next emergency faster to contain.

When an electrician recommends a panel upgrade, ask what evidence supports it: measured demand, service rating, available spaces, damaged buswork, obsolete equipment, or planned loads. A panel replacement should include correct grounding, labeling, protection, and coordination with the local authority where required. If the panel is healthy, a dedicated circuit or load management may be the whole job.

When to Call

When to Call a Licensed Electrician

  • The same breaker trips repeatedly after reducing the load
  • Breaker will not reset, trips immediately, or will not leave the middle position
  • Burning smell, buzzing sound, or a warm breaker panel
  • Scorch marks, melted plastic, or discoloration at the panel or a receptacle
  • Multiple breakers tripping together, which usually points upstream of the branch circuits
  • A trip that followed water intrusion or a storm, even if things look fine now

Guessing wrong here is expensive in both directions: calling too early costs a diagnostic fee, waiting too long leaves a live fault in the wall. The safety warning list in the FAQ separates the two.

Practical takeaway

Do Not Treat the Breaker as the Problem by Default

The breaker is usually the messenger. Replacing it without finding the reason for the trip can leave an overloaded conductor, a damaged receptacle, or a failing appliance in service. A proper repair restores protection rather than defeating it.

If the trip happened once on a clearly overloaded circuit and the circuit runs normally after the loads are reduced, monitor it and avoid repeating the overload. If it repeats, changes pattern, or involves heat or odor, schedule diagnosis. When you call, describe the panel brand if you can see it safely, the rooms affected, what was running, and whether the breaker is standard, GFCI, or AFCI. That detail lets the electrician arrive with the right test equipment and parts in the van.

Prevent repeat trips

Practical Prevention for LA Homes

Spread high loads

Do not run multiple portable heaters or cooking appliances on one circuit, even if the breaker holds. Use properly rated extension cords temporarily only, never as permanent wiring.

Maintain wet-area protection

Test GFCI devices on the schedule the manufacturer or the device itself specifies, and investigate trips instead of repeatedly resetting them.

Plan upgrades

Before an EV charger or major appliance arrives, review panel capacity and put in a properly sized dedicated circuit. See our EV charger installation service and dedicated circuits.

Questions

Breaker Tripping FAQ

Why does my circuit breaker keep tripping? ▾
The three most common causes are an overloaded circuit (too many devices drawing more than the circuit rating), a short circuit (a hot conductor contacting neutral), and a ground fault (a conductor contacting a grounded surface, often through moisture or damaged insulation). Arc faults, failing breakers, and new loads beyond capacity explain most of the rest. A trip is the protection working: it prevents wire overheating and fire.
How do I reset a tripped circuit breaker? ▾
Push the handle firmly to OFF first, tripped breakers rest in a middle position and will not re-engage from there, then flip it fully to ON. If it holds, unplug the heaviest loads that shared the circuit and redistribute them. If it trips again immediately, or trips with no load connected, leave it OFF and call a licensed electrician. Never tape, wedge, or hold a breaker on, and never install a higher-amperage breaker.
Should I install a bigger breaker? ▾
No. The breaker must match the conductor size and the equipment it protects. A larger breaker allows wiring to overheat before it trips, which converts a nuisance into a fire risk. If the circuit is genuinely overloaded, the fix is redistributing loads, a dedicated circuit, or a load calculation for a panel upgrade.
Can I fix a tripping breaker myself? ▾
You can safely do what an unopened panel allows: unplug loads, reset once, test GFCI receptacles, and read the pattern. Do not remove the panel cover, replace a breaker, or tighten terminals. If the breaker trips with no load, will not reset, or the trip involves heat, odor, or water, that part is licensed-electrician territory.
How much does it cost to fix a breaker problem in Los Angeles? ▾
A diagnostic service call runs $95 to $195. Replacing a single-pole breaker runs $150 to $350, a double-pole breaker $200 to $500, and a GFCI or AFCI breaker $250 to $450. Rewiring a faulty circuit runs $300 to $800. Prices include labor, parts, and permit where applicable, and the diagnostic fee exists because most breaker faults are not at the breaker itself.
Can an EV charger cause breaker trips? ▾
Yes. The circuit may be undersized, shared with other loads, or improperly configured. EV charging requires a designed, dedicated, and inspected circuit sized to the charger, and a load calculation confirms whether the panel can carry it. If your trips started when the charger went in, that circuit is the first place to look.
When is a tripping breaker an emergency? ▾
Call 911 for fire or injury. Call an electrician the same day for smoke or a burning smell, buzzing, melted insulation, a hot panel, water near the equipment, or a breaker that will not stay on. A trip without any of those signs can follow the safe reset in this guide; it becomes urgent the moment any of those signs appear.
