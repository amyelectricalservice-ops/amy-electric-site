Why Your Breaker Keeps Tripping:
Causes & Fixes

By Amy, Licensed C-10 Electrician · Serving Los Angeles

A circuit breaker that trips is telling you something. Sometimes it is a simple overload; sometimes it is the only warning before damaged wiring creates a fire or shock hazard. This guide helps LA homeowners respond safely without guessing.

Start safely

What a Tripping Breaker Is Designed to Do

A breaker opens the circuit when current exceeds a safe level or when its protective electronics detect an arc or ground fault. It protects the conductors inside your walls, not just the appliance at the end of the circuit. A trip is therefore useful information, not an inconvenience to defeat.

Before touching anything, look and listen. Smoke, flames, a sharp burning odor, buzzing, a warm panel, visible scorching, water near electrical equipment, or anyone receiving a shock changes this from troubleshooting to an emergency. Leave the area and call for emergency electrical guidance; call 911 for fire or injury.

Common causes

Six Reasons Breakers Trip

1. Circuit overload

A 15- or 20-amp branch circuit can be overloaded when several high-draw devices run together. Portable heaters, hair dryers, microwaves, irons, vacuums, and window air conditioners are common contributors. In older LA homes, one circuit may serve more rooms than a homeowner realizes.

2. Short circuit

A damaged hot conductor touching neutral creates a sudden current surge. A crushed cord, failed receptacle, loose terminal, or nail through a cable can be involved. The breaker may trip instantly when a device is plugged in or switched on.

3. Ground fault

Current escaping to a metal box, grounding conductor, wet surface, or appliance frame can trip a GFCI or AFCI. Kitchens, bathrooms, garages, patios, pools, and laundry areas deserve extra caution because moisture increases shock risk.

4. Arc fault

Loose connections, damaged insulation, or a failing device can create an electrical arc. AFCI protection is intended to detect this pattern. Do not bypass an AFCI because nuisance trips may be signaling a real wiring or appliance defect.

5. Failing breaker or panel

Breakers can wear out, fail to latch, or develop heat damage. A panel with corrosion, double-tapped conductors, obsolete equipment, or a history of overheating needs a professional inspection rather than a larger breaker.

6. New load exceeds available capacity

Adding an EV charger, heat-pump equipment, induction range, spa, or workshop tools can expose a capacity problem. An electrician should perform a load calculation before adding a circuit. A panel upgrade may be appropriate, but it is not the answer to every trip.

Safe homeowner check

What You Can Check Without Opening the Panel

  1. Identify the affected area. Note which rooms, outlets, lights, or appliances lost power. Check whether a GFCI receptacle has tripped and reset it only if it is dry and undamaged.
  2. Unplug portable loads. Remove heaters, cooking appliances, tools, power strips, and chargers from the affected circuit. Do not move a suspected damaged appliance to another outlet.
  3. Reset once. Stand to the side, keep hands dry, move the handle fully OFF, then ON. If it trips immediately, stop. Never hold the handle on or install a higher-rated breaker.
  4. Observe the pattern. A trip only when several devices run suggests overload; an instant trip with one appliance suggests a fault. Either pattern can require testing.
  5. Document clues. Record the time, weather, appliances operating, and any odor or sound. This helps an electrician diagnose the issue faster.

Never remove the panel cover. Parts inside can remain energized even when the main breaker is off. Do not probe outlets, tighten terminals, or replace a breaker unless you are qualified and authorized to do that work.

Professional diagnosis

How an Electrician Finds the Fault

A licensed electrician starts with a visual inspection and interviews you about the trip pattern. They may test voltage, continuity, insulation condition, GFCI/AFCI operation, receptacles, appliance connections, and circuit loading. Thermal imaging can reveal abnormal heat, while circuit tracing identifies which outlets and fixtures share a breaker.

The repair might be as focused as replacing a damaged receptacle or cord, or as extensive as correcting a loose splice, separating overloaded loads, rewiring a branch circuit, or replacing a heat-damaged panel. The correct fix preserves the existing conductor size and protective-device rating. If the home has outdated wiring, a broader rewiring evaluation may be recommended without assuming the entire house must be rewired.

For permitted work, requirements depend on the city and scope. In Los Angeles, confirm current permit and inspection expectations with LADBS or the applicable local building department. A written estimate should explain the diagnostic findings, repair options, and any work that is outside the initial visit.

Prevent repeat trips

Practical Prevention for LA Homes

Spread high loads

Do not run multiple portable heaters or cooking appliances on one circuit. Use only properly rated extension cords temporarily, never as permanent wiring.

Maintain wet-area protection

Test GFCI devices according to the manufacturer’s instructions and investigate trips instead of repeatedly resetting them.

Plan upgrades

Before an EV charger or major appliance, review panel capacity and install a properly sized dedicated circuit. See our EV charger installation service.

A closer look

Patterns That Help Explain a Trip

A breaker that trips after several minutes of heating is behaving differently from one that trips the instant a switch is turned on. Time-delayed trips often point toward sustained overload or heat at a loose connection. Instant trips can point toward a short, ground fault, or a device with an internal failure. These clues help a professional choose tests, but they are not a diagnosis by themselves.

Pay attention to whether the problem follows one appliance or stays with one outlet. If a portable device trips multiple known-good circuits, stop using it and have the device serviced or replaced. If several devices work elsewhere but one receptacle trips the circuit, the receptacle, box, or branch wiring may be involved. Do not keep moving a questionable appliance around the house to “test” it.

Seasonal conditions can change the pattern. Summer air conditioners and pool equipment add long-running loads; winter heaters and holiday lighting add temporary demand. In coastal areas, moisture and corrosion can affect outdoor receptacles. In older neighborhoods, renovations may have left circuits with confusing labels. A circuit directory that is corrected after repair is a small improvement that makes the next emergency safer.

When an electrician recommends a panel upgrade, ask what evidence supports it: measured demand, service rating, available spaces, damaged buswork, obsolete equipment, or planned loads. A panel replacement should include correct grounding, labeling, protection, and coordination with the local authority when required. If the panel is healthy, a dedicated circuit, load management, or a smaller repair may be enough.

A practical takeaway

Do Not Treat the Breaker as the Problem by Default

The breaker is often the messenger. Replacing it without finding the reason for the trip can leave an overloaded conductor, damaged receptacle, or failing appliance in service. A proper repair restores protection rather than defeating it. If a trip happened once after a clearly overloaded circuit and the circuit works normally after loads are reduced, monitor it and avoid repeating the overload. If the event repeats, changes pattern, or involves heat or odor, schedule diagnosis. When you call, describe the panel label, rooms affected, what was running, and whether the breaker is standard, GFCI, or AFCI if you can identify it safely. That information lets the electrician bring appropriate test equipment and parts.

FAQ

Breaker Tripping Questions

Why does my breaker keep tripping? ▾
Repeated trips usually indicate an overload, short circuit, ground fault, failing breaker, or wiring problem. Remove loads and call a licensed electrician if it trips again.
Can I keep resetting it? ▾
No. Reset once after removing loads only when there is no heat, smoke, water, or burning smell. Repeated resets can energize a dangerous fault.
Should I install a bigger breaker? ▾
Never. The breaker must match the conductor and equipment. A larger breaker can allow wiring to overheat.
Can an EV charger cause trips? ▾
Yes. The circuit may be undersized, shared, or improperly configured. EV charging requires a designed and inspected dedicated circuit.
When is this an emergency? ▾
Call 911 for fire or injury. Call an electrician promptly for smoke, arcing, melted insulation, a hot panel, water intrusion, or a breaker that will not stay on.
