Smart Home Electrical Requirements: What Your Wiring Needs

Smart homes need more than Wi-Fi. Behind every voice command and automated scene is electrical infrastructure: neutrals at switches, capacity at the panel, and reliable dedicated circuits. Here's what LA homeowners should plan for.

🤖

Neutral at Every Switch

Smart switches, dimmers, and timers need a neutral wire. Many older LA homes only ran hot and traveler - we add neutrals during upgrades.

🔌

Panel Capacity

Smart panels, EV chargers, and battery storage add load. A 200-amp panel gives headroom for a connected home.

📡

Dedicated Circuits

Network racks, PoE switches, and server closets benefit from dedicated, surge-protected circuits to avoid downtime.

📱

Structured Wiring

Running Cat6 alongside electrical during a remodel future-proofs automation, cameras, and access control.

Plan Ahead

When to Upgrade for Smart Home

1
Before a remodel

Run neutrals, Cat6, and extra conduit while walls are open - retrofitting later is far costlier.

2
During a panel upgrade

Specify smart-panel compatibility and spare breaker spaces for future devices.

3
Whole-home rewire

The ideal time to build a fully smart-ready electrical backbone.

By Device

What Common Smart-Home Devices Actually Need

Most smart-home devices are low-power and run on existing circuits. The electrical work is not in the device — it is in the wiring behind the switch and the capacity at the panel. Here is what each category actually requires:

DeviceElectrical requirementCommon gotcha
Smart switches and dimmersNeutral at the switch box (most modern code requires it); derating for LED loadsOlder homes have no neutral at the switch — requires a rewire of that circuit
Smart thermostatsC-wire (common wire) at the thermostat location; 24V control wiringMany LA homes have 4-wire thermostat cable with no C-wire; a power-stealing adapter or a new cable is needed
Smart panels (Span, etc.)Full panel replacement; 200A service; Wi-Fi at the panel locationNot a retrofit — it is a panel upgrade with smart breakers
EV charger (smart)Dedicated 40–60A 240V circuit; load management if panel is tightSmart chargers can load-share, which sometimes avoids a panel upgrade
Motorized shades and blindsLow-voltage power at each window; often a dedicated 120V circuit for the power supplyWiring has to be in place before drywall is closed
Smart lighting (Lutron, etc.)Neutral at switches; sometimes a central processor location with power and dataWhole-home systems need a plan before renovation, not after
Video doorbells and camerasLow-voltage at the door; transformer at the chime or panelOlder homes have no doorbell transformer, or it is undersized
Whole-home audioSpeaker wire (low voltage) + a powered amplifier locationNot electrical work per se, but it shares walls with the electrical rough-in
Wired vs Wireless

Wi-Fi Smart Home vs Hardwired: The Electrical Difference

Most consumer smart-home devices are wireless (Wi-Fi, Zigbee, Z-Wave, Matter). The electrical work is limited to the switch or outlet they replace. A hardwired system (Lutron Homeworks, Control4, Savant) is different: it runs low-voltage cable to every device and a processor, and the electrical rough-in has to be planned during construction or renovation.

The practical difference for the homeowner: wireless can be retrofitted into an existing house with minimal electrical work; hardwired cannot. If you are renovating, the cost difference between running low-voltage cable while walls are open and fishing it later is enormous, and the second option is often impossible.

What a smart-home-ready panel looks like

  • 200A service with open breaker space for future circuits
  • AFCI and GFCI protection on the circuits that need it (smart devices are sensitive to nuisance trips)
  • A dedicated circuit for the network rack, the AV processor, and any motorized equipment
  • Clean, labeled directory so the next electrician can find things
  • Surge protection at the panel, because smart devices are electronics first
Mistakes

Common Mistakes We See in LA Smart-Home Projects

  • No neutral at the switch. The single most common reason a smart switch "doesn't work" in an older LA home. The fix is a neutral run or a no-neutral-rated switch, and the first option requires opening the wall.
  • Overloading a circuit with smart plugs. A smart plug does not reduce the load. Ten smart plugs on a 15A circuit is still a 15A circuit.
  • Skipping the panel assessment. Adding a smart panel, an EV charger, and a heat pump to a 100A service is a recipe for nuisance trips. Do the load calculation before buying equipment.
  • Running low-voltage cable after drywall. Possible with fishing, but slow and expensive. Plan it during renovation.
  • Ignoring surge protection. Smart homes are electronics-heavy homes. A panel-level SPD is cheap insurance against the surges that come with LA's grid.
Questions

Smart Home Electrical FAQ

Why do smart switches need a neutral wire? ▾
The switch itself draws a small amount of current to stay powered and communicate. Without a neutral at the box, many smart switches won't function.
Can I make my old home smart-ready? ▾
Yes. We add neutrals to key switches, install a 200-amp panel with spare capacity, and run network cabling during targeted upgrades.
Do smart homes need more panel capacity? ▾
Often. EV chargers, battery storage, and smart panels add load. We calculate total demand before recommending a size.
Is structured wiring still worth it with Wi-Fi? ▾
Yes for reliability. Cameras, access points, and automation hubs perform better on wired backbones than crowded Wi-Fi.
Should I get a smart electrical panel? ▾
If you want circuit-level monitoring and load control, a smart panel is compelling - but it requires a modern, adequately sized service to start.
Do smart home devices need a permit in Los Angeles? ▾
The devices themselves do not, but the electrical work behind them does. Adding circuits, replacing a panel, or running new cable all require an LADBS permit and inspection. Low-voltage work (speaker wire, data cable) generally does not require a permit, but it should be coordinated with the electrical rough-in so the walls are opened once.
Can I add smart switches to a house with no neutral at the switches? ▾
Sometimes. Some smart switches are rated for no-neutral installations, but they are less reliable and do not work with all loads. The better fix is running a neutral to the switch box, which requires opening the wall and fishing cable. An inspection tells you which switches in your house have neutrals and which do not.
