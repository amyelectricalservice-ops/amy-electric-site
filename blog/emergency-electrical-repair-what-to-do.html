Emergency Electrical Repair:
What to Do

By Amy, California C-10 Contractor #981578

Electrical problems can become dangerous quickly. This Los Angeles-focused guide explains how to protect people first, identify the right responder, and preserve useful information for a safe repair.

First priority

Protect People Before Property

If you see flames, heavy smoke, an electrical explosion, or someone has been shocked, move everyone away and call 911. Do not touch a person who may still be in contact with electricity until power is safely disconnected. Tell the dispatcher the address, what happened, whether anyone is injured, and whether fire or smoke is visible.

For any situation, keep children, pets, and bystanders away. Do not handle a sparking receptacle, pull a damaged cord, open a panel cover, or walk through water near electrical equipment. A “quick fix” can turn a controllable fault into a fire or electrocution.

Who to call

911, LADWP, or an Electrician?

Call 911

Call emergency services for active fire, smoke that is spreading, an explosion, a serious electrical shock, a person who is unconscious or injured, or a downed line threatening people or traffic. Evacuate, close doors if safe, and never re-enter because a breaker appears off. Firefighters can coordinate scene safety with the utility.

LADWP or the utility

Contact LADWP when the neighborhood is out, a service pole or overhead line is damaged, a wire is down, or the issue appears to be on the utility side of the meter. Assume every downed line is energized. Keep at least a full vehicle length away, warn others, and report its location. Do not trim trees near wires or attempt to move a cable.

A licensed electrician

Call a licensed electrician for a home-only outage after utility conditions are checked, repeated breaker trips, a hot or buzzing panel, burning odor, melted devices, failed GFCI/AFCI protection, damaged branch wiring, or water intrusion after the scene is declared safe. Safe steps

What to Do While Help Is Coming

  1. Leave danger zones. Move outside if there is smoke, fire, a strong burning odor, crackling, or an unstable ceiling or wall.
  2. Disconnect only when safe. If you can reach the breaker without entering smoke or water, switch off the affected circuit. Never approach a wet, damaged, or arcing panel.
  3. Use a phone from a safe location. Give responders the exact address, cross streets, gated-access details, and a clear description. Do not delay 911 to find a contractor.
  4. Preserve the scene. Do not discard a failed charger, appliance, breaker, or cord. Photograph damage only from a safe distance and keep receipts for an insurer.
  5. Wait for clearance. After fire, flooding, or utility work, power should be restored only after the responsible professionals confirm the equipment is safe.

Never use water on an electrical fire. If you are trained, have a clear exit, and the fire is small, a Class C or multipurpose extinguisher may be appropriate; otherwise evacuate immediately.

Common LA emergencies

Situations That Need Prompt Attention

Sparking outlet

Switch off the circuit if safe, unplug nothing from the damaged receptacle, and arrange a professional inspection. Sparks can indicate loose terminals, arcing, or a failing device.

Burning panel smell

Keep clear and do not remove the cover. Heat damage can involve bus bars or service conductors. Call 911 for smoke or fire, then an electrician once the scene is safe.

Storm or wildfire damage

Los Angeles wind, fire, and flooding can damage service masts, conduits, meters, and branch circuits. Utility and building-department coordination may be required before reconnection.

Home-only outage

Check neighbors from a safe location and look for an obvious tripped breaker. If resetting once does not restore power, stop and call for diagnosis rather than forcing the main.

After the emergency

Repair, Documentation, and Prevention

Once immediate danger has passed, ask for a written diagnosis. It should distinguish the failed component from secondary damage, note whether utility equipment is involved, and identify any permit or inspection needs. Do not accept a larger fuse or breaker as a substitute for correcting wiring, load, or equipment problems.

If water entered a panel, conduit, receptacle, or wall, drying alone does not prove the installation is safe. Corrosion and insulation damage can appear later. Ask the electrician to document what was inspected and what must be replaced. If a panel is obsolete or lacks capacity for modern loads, discuss a panel upgrade; if circuits are deteriorated throughout an older home, compare repair with whole-home rewiring.

For future resilience, label circuits, test GFCI devices, maintain clear access to the panel, keep combustibles away from electrical equipment, and have EV chargers and generators installed on correctly designed circuits. Local rules vary across LA jurisdictions, so confirm permits and inspections with LADBS or your city.

Be prepared

Build an Emergency Electrical Plan

Keep the utility outage number, 911, your electrician, and your insurance contact in your phone before a problem occurs. Make sure the panel is reachable and that adults in the household know where it is. A simple diagram identifying the main disconnect, gas shutoff, water shutoff, and solar or battery disconnect can help responders, but never delay evacuation to search for it.

Use flashlights rather than candles during an outage, especially near gas appliances or damaged wiring. Unplug sensitive electronics if there is a surge or repeated flicker, then leave refrigerators and freezers closed as much as possible. Never connect a portable generator to a wall outlet or indoor space. Carbon monoxide can build up quickly; generators belong outdoors, away from doors, windows, and vents, following the manufacturer’s instructions and local requirements.

After an emergency repair, ask what warning signs should trigger a return visit. A replacement breaker should not be treated as proof that the underlying problem is gone. If an appliance caused the failure, obtain its model and serial number for records. If the event involved fire, water, or a utility disconnect, retain inspection reports and photographs for your insurer and future electrical work.

Los Angeles properties vary from apartments and hillside homes to older single-family houses and commercial spaces. Access, shared meters, solar equipment, and local jurisdiction can change the response. An electrician can coordinate the electrical portion, but only the utility can address its equipment and only emergency responders can manage an active life-safety scene.

Communication

What to Tell the Responder

A concise description helps the right professional respond. Keep the description factual rather than guessing at the cause, and mention whether anyone is still inside or whether power has already been disconnected. Tell 911 whether there is fire, smoke, injury, or a downed line. If you smell gas as well, report that separately and avoid switches or flames. Tell the utility whether neighbors are also out and where a line or pole appears damaged. Tell an electrician which breaker or GFCI operated, what appliances were running, when the symptom began, and whether water, construction, or storm damage is involved. Mention solar panels, batteries, generators, EV chargers, or shared meters before anyone begins work. Do not send someone into a dangerous area to take pictures. From a safe location, note the address, access instructions, pets, tenants, and any utility or fire-department incident number. Clear communication reduces repeat visits and helps keep everyone out of the hazard zone.

FAQ

Emergency Repair FAQ

When should I call 911? ▾
Call for fire, visible smoke, explosion, serious shock, injury, or a downed line threatening people. Evacuate and do not re-enter until responders say it is safe.
When should I call LADWP? ▾
Call the utility for neighborhood outages, downed utility lines, damaged poles, or suspected utility-side meter problems. Treat every downed line as energized.
When should I call an electrician? ▾
Call for home-only outages, repeated trips, burning odors, hot panels, sparking devices, damaged wiring, or water near electrical equipment after immediate hazards are controlled.
Can I use water on an electrical fire? ▾
No. Water conducts electricity. Evacuate and call 911; use an appropriate extinguisher only if trained and you have a clear exit.
What after flooding? ▾
Stay away from water and equipment, call 911 if anyone is at risk, contact the utility when its equipment may be affected, and arrange an electrician inspection before power is restored.
