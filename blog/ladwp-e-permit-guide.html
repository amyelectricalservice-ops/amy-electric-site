Quick Summary

What Is the LADBS e-Permit System?

The Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety (LADBS) operates an online e-permit portal that allows licensed contractors to apply for electrical permits, upload plans, pay fees, and schedule inspections entirely online. While many Los Angeles homeowners refer to it as the LADWP e-permit system, it is actually LADBS that manages the building and safety permitting process — LADWP handles the utility side (meter installations and service connections) separately.

As a licensed C-10 electrical contractor (License #981578) with over a decade of experience, I have submitted hundreds of e-permits through LADBS across multiple Los Angeles jurisdictions. This guide walks you through every step of the process — what documents you need, how much it costs, how long it takes, and what to do if your application hits a snag.

Step-by-Step

How to Get an Electrical Permit Through LADBS e-Permit

Step 1: Determine Your Permit Type

Before you apply, identify the correct LADBS permit classification for your project. Most residential electrical work falls under a standard electrical permit, which covers panel upgrades, EV charger installations, new circuits, and service changes. Complex projects combining electrical work with structural modifications require a building permit with an electrical sub-permit. Your licensed contractor will know the correct classification — this is one reason why working with an experienced electrician saves time during permitting.

Step 2: Gather Your Documents

A complete e-permit application requires several supporting documents. These include your current C-10 contractor license (on file with LADBS), workers compensation certificate, general liability insurance certificate, load calculations per NEC Article 220, a one-line riser diagram showing the electrical system configuration, equipment specification sheets (panel label, EV charger specs, etc.), and a site plan showing equipment locations. Missing documents are the most common reason for application rejection.

Step 3: Submit Through the e-Permit Portal

Licensed contractors register for an account on the LADBS e-permit portal at ladbs.org. The application requires the project address (within Los Angeles city limits), a detailed scope of work description, total project valuation, and contractor license information. Upload all supporting documents as PDFs. The portal provides real-time status updates and allows you to track your application through the review process.

Step 4: Pay Permit Fees

Once your application is submitted, the portal calculates the permit fee based on your project valuation and permit type. Typical fees include a permit issuance fee, plan review fee (for projects requiring plan check), and inspection fees. Payment is made online via credit card or electronic check. For straightforward permits like EV charger installations, you typically pay between $75 and $200 total.

Step 5: Complete Work and Schedule Rough-In Inspection

Once the permit is issued, your electrician can begin work. After the wiring, conduit, boxes, and panel are installed — but before any drywall or finish work — the rough-in inspection is scheduled through the e-permit portal. The LADBS inspector verifies wire gauge, conduit sizing and fill, box fill limits, grounding continuity, and proper installation methods per the California Electrical Code.

Step 6: Final Inspection and Permit Closure

After all devices, switches, outlets, panel covers, and fixtures are installed and operational, the final inspection is scheduled. The inspector tests GFCI and AFCI functionality, verifies proper breaker sizing, checks bonding and grounding connections, and confirms the completed work matches the permitted scope. Once approved, LADBS closes the permit and issues a Certificate of Completion — keep this document with your home records as proof of code-compliant electrical work.

Step 7: LADWP Meter Release (Service Upgrades Only)

If your project involves a service upgrade or panel change that requires LADWP to disconnect and reconnect power, a separate LADWP meter release inspection is required. This inspection happens after LADBS final approval. LADWP verifies the meter socket, service conductors, and main disconnect before re-energizing. The meter release process typically adds 2-5 business days to the project timeline.

Common Mistakes

Top Reasons LADBS e-Permit Applications Get Rejected

After processing hundreds of e-permit applications through LADBS, here are the most common rejection reasons and how to avoid them.

Incomplete Load Calculations

Missing or incorrect load calculations per NEC Article 220 are the #1 rejection reason. Every electrical permit requires a detailed load calculation showing the existing and new loads on your panel.

Wrong Permit Classification

Applying under the wrong permit type — e.g., using a standard electrical permit for work that requires a building permit with plan review — causes immediate rejection. Your contractor must know the correct classification.

Expired License or Insurance

LADBS validates that your contractor's C-10 license, workers comp, and liability insurance are current before processing any permit. Expired credentials cause automatic rejection until renewed and uploaded.

Missing Equipment Specs

EV chargers, panels, and major equipment require manufacturer specification sheets uploaded with the application. Generic descriptions without model numbers and spec sheets will be rejected.

Valuation Mismatch

Permit fees are calculated based on the stated project valuation. Listing a valuation that is significantly below market rates triggers a review flag and potential rejection or additional fee assessment.

Scope Description Too Vague

"Install EV charger" or "Upgrade panel" is not sufficient. LADBS requires a detailed scope description including equipment model numbers, circuit sizes, conduit types, and specific installation locations.

Other Jurisdictions

e-Permit Systems in Other LA-Area Cities

Not all Los Angeles area electrical work falls under LADBS. Several cities operate their own building departments with separate permitting systems. Here is how the major ones work:

🏛

Burbank

Burbank Building Division issues electrical permits. Separate from LADBS. Online application available. Most permits issued within 48 hours. Plan review required for service upgrades.

🏛

Glendale

Glendale Building & Safety permits electrical work through their online portal. Similar requirements to LADBS. Typical turnaround 24-72 hours for standard permits.

🏛

Pasadena

Pasadena Building & Safety Division handles its own permitting. Online application system. Slightly different fee structure than LADBS — plan ahead for accurate cost estimates.

🏛

Santa Monica

Santa Monica permits go through the City of Santa Monica Building & Safety. Known for thorough plan review. Budget 1-2 weeks longer than LADBS for complex permits.

🏛

Beverly Hills

Beverly Hills Community Development processes permits separately. More stringent requirements for historic district properties. Coordination with the city's design review may be required.

🏛

Culver City

Culver City Community Development handles permits. Smaller department means fewer reviewers — plan for 3-5 business day turnaround on standard permits, longer for plan review.

As a licensed contractor serving all sixteen Greater Los Angeles cities, we maintain active registrations with each city's building department and handle the specific permitting requirements of every jurisdiction.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions About LADBS e-Permits

What is the LADWP e-permit system for electrical work? ▾
The LADWP e-permit system is actually managed by the Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety (LADBS), not LADWP itself — a common point of confusion among Los Angeles homeowners. LADBS operates an online permitting portal that allows licensed contractors to apply for electrical permits, submit plans, schedule inspections, and pay fees entirely online. LADWP handles the utility connection (service upgrades, meter installations) and requires separate coordination. For most residential electrical work — including panel upgrades, EV charger installations, and new circuits — the LADBS e-permit portal is your single point of entry. As a licensed C-10 electrical contractor, AMY Electric manages the entire LADBS e-permit process for every project we perform, from initial application through final inspection sign-off.
Do I need an e-permit to install an EV charger? ▾
Yes — all EV charger installations in the City of Los Angeles require a permit through the LADBS e-permit system. This applies to both Level 2 chargers (240V, hardwired or plug-in) and any new dedicated circuit installation. The permit ensures the work meets current California Electrical Code requirements, including proper conduit sizing, GFCI protection, load calculations, and bonding requirements. An unpermitted EV charger installation can create significant problems: your homeowners insurance may deny claims related to the charger, the installation will not be inspected for safety, and it must be disclosed when selling your home. A licensed electrician pulls the permit, schedules the mandatory rough-in and final inspections, and obtains the Certificate of Completion.
How much does an LADBS e-permit cost? ▾
LADBS e-permit fees vary based on the scope and valuation of the electrical work. Typical permit fee ranges for common residential projects include: EV charger installation at $75 to $200, panel upgrade (100A to 200A) at $150 to $400, new dedicated circuit at $50 to $150, whole-home rewire at $200 to $600, and service upgrade (overhead to underground) at $300 to $800. These fees include the permit issuance, plan review, and two inspections (rough-in and final). Additional fees may apply for express plan review, after-hours inspections, or projects requiring structural engineering review. Your contractor should include all permit fees in their written estimate — never pay these separately.
How long does it take to get an electrical permit through LADBS? ▾
The LADBS e-permit timeline depends on the complexity of the project. Standard over-the-counter electrical permits for straightforward work like EV charger installations or panel replacements are typically issued within 24 to 72 hours of application. Projects requiring plan review — such as service upgrades, main panel relocations, or complex commercial work — take 2 to 6 weeks for LADBS to review and approve. The fastest way to get your permit is to work with a licensed contractor who knows how to submit complete, accurate applications with the correct permit classifications, load calculations, and supporting documentation. Incomplete or incorrect applications are the most common cause of permitting delays, often adding weeks to the process.
Can a homeowner apply for an e-permit? ▾
Homeowners in Los Angeles can apply for an owner-builder permit through LADBS for electrical work on their own single-family dwelling, provided they live in the home and perform the work themselves. However, the owner-builder permit route carries significant drawbacks: you are personally liable for code compliance, you must schedule and attend all inspections yourself, and any work you perform that fails inspection becomes your responsibility to fix. Most homeowners find that hiring a licensed C-10 electrical contractor who handles the entire e-permit process is far simpler and safer. The contractor's license, bond, and insurance are on file with LADBS, which streamlines the application process and ensures the work meets all code requirements.
What inspections are required after getting an e-permit? ▾
Most electrical permits in Los Angeles require at least two inspections. The rough-in inspection occurs after the wiring, conduit, and boxes are installed but before walls are closed — the inspector verifies proper wire gauge, secure connections, conduit fill compliance, box fill limits, and grounding continuity. The final inspection occurs after all devices, panels, and fixtures are installed and operational — the inspector tests GFCI and AFCI function, verifies proper breaker sizing, checks bonding and grounding, and confirms all work matches the permitted scope. For panel upgrades and service changes, LADWP also requires a separate meter release inspection before the utility can reconnect power. Passing both LADBS inspections is required to close the permit and obtain the Certificate of Completion.
What happens if my e-permit application is rejected? ▾
LADBS e-permit applications are typically rejected for one of several common reasons: missing or incomplete load calculations, incorrect permit classification for the scope of work, missing supporting documents (site plans, one-line diagrams, manufacturer specs), expired contractor license on file with LADBS, or valuation discrepancies between the stated project cost and the fee calculation. When an application is rejected, LADBS provides a correction notice specifying exactly what needs to be revised. You can resubmit the corrected application through the e-permit portal without starting over. Working with a licensed contractor who submits e-permits regularly dramatically reduces the rejection rate — experienced contractors know exactly what LADBS expects for each permit type and include all required documentation in the initial submission.
